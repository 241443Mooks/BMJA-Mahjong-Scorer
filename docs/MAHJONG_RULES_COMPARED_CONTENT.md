# Publishable page copy

# British vs Hong Kong vs Riichi vs Chinese Official vs American Mahjong

## Same family of tiles. Five very different ways to play.

If two people say they play Mahjong, they may not be talking about quite the same game.

British Mahjong, Hong Kong Mahjong, Japanese Riichi, Chinese Official Mahjong and American Mah Jongg share a common ancestry and many familiar tiles. But the rules can differ in important ways: what counts as a legal winning hand, how Chows work, whether Flowers are used, which patterns matter, how scores are calculated, and even whether you need an annually changing card beside you.

This guide compares five major rulesets at a practical level. It is designed to help you answer two questions:

1. **Which kind of Mahjong am I looking at?**
2. **What will feel different if I move from one ruleset to another?**

If you are looking for the rules used by this scorer, they are **British Mahjong rules based on the BMJA tradition**. The scorer does not calculate Hong Kong, Riichi, MCR or American scores.

# The quickest way to identify your Mahjong rules

## Do you have Jokers and an annually changing card of legal hands?

You are probably playing **American Mah Jongg**, particularly NMJL-style play.

The National Mah Jongg League publishes a new Standard Hands and Rules card every year. Players build one of the patterns shown on that card. American play also uses Jokers and begins with the Charleston, a structured exchange of unwanted tiles.

**Strong clue:** if someone says, “Is that hand on this year's card?”, you are not playing British Mahjong.

---

## Can a player declare Riichi, and are people talking about yaku, han, fu, dora or furiten?

You are playing **Japanese Riichi Mahjong**.

A structurally complete hand is not automatically enough to win: it needs at least one scoring condition called a **yaku**. Riichi itself is one possible yaku: a concealed player in tenpai can make a declaration and commit to the hand. Dora can increase a winning hand's value but does not by itself make an otherwise yaku-less hand legal.

Furiten — restrictions on winning from another player's discard in particular circumstances — gives Riichi a distinctive defensive dimension.

---

## Are you using a formal international competition system with a large standard catalogue of scoring combinations and an 8-point minimum?

You are probably playing **Mahjong Competition Rules (MCR)**, often called **Chinese Official** or **Chinese International** Mahjong.

MCR was designed as a standardised competition ruleset. Instead of relying on a local family or club scoring table, players combine recognised scoring patterns under a common tournament framework. The official system is substantially more catalogue-heavy than ordinary British scoring.

---

## Does ordinary play use faan/fan scoring, normal Chow/Pung/Kong structure and Cantonese/Hong Kong conventions?

You may be playing **Hong Kong Mahjong**.

Hong Kong Mahjong keeps the familiar draw-discard rhythm and ordinary four-sets-and-a-pair structure. Scoring is typically expressed using **faan** (also written fan), but exact minimums and payment tables vary between groups and published Hong Kong rulesets.

This is one reason “Chinese Mahjong” is not precise enough to identify a scoring system: China and Chinese-speaking communities contain many different regional traditions.

---

## Do Flowers and Seasons score, non-winners' hands can matter, and an ordinary hand is limited to one Chow?

Those are strong signs of **British Mahjong in the BMJA tradition**.

British scoring gives point values to Pungs, Kongs, certain pairs, Flowers and Seasons, then applies doubles. The one-Chow restriction is especially distinctive: unlike Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR, an ordinary British grouped hand cannot simply contain several Chows.

British play also has a catalogue of named special hands and special fishing values. After Mah-Jong, the other players' hand values can still matter because the non-winners settle differences between their scores as part of the British settlement system.

---

# What all five still have in common

The variants are different, but they are not unrelated games invented from scratch.

Across British, Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR you will immediately recognise:

- Characters, Bamboos and Circles/Dots;
- Winds and Dragons;
- Pungs/triplets;
- Kongs/quads;
- Chows/sequences;
- drawing and discarding;
- a four-player table as the standard format; and
- the familiar 13-tile hand that becomes 14 tiles when an ordinary winning tile is added.

American Mah Jongg uses much of the same tile vocabulary and draw/discard ancestry, but its annual card changes the target structures so substantially that experience with another variant does not tell you which American hands are currently legal.

The useful mental model is:

> **Mahjong is a family of related rulesets, not one universal rulebook with a few house rules.**

# British Mahjong

## What makes British Mahjong distinctive?

The British rules used by this project are based on the BMJA tradition. An ordinary hand still uses familiar Pungs, Kongs, Chows and a pair, but several details make the scoring system recognisably British.

### One Chow in an ordinary hand

A normal grouped British hand may contain **at most one Chow**. This is one of the fastest ways to discover that a tutorial written for Hong Kong or Riichi is not teaching the same game you play.

### Individual sets have point values

British scoring distinguishes **major** tiles from **minor** tiles. Pungs and Kongs score different base points according to tile class and whether the set is exposed or concealed. Certain honour pairs score too.

### Flowers and Seasons matter

Flowers and Seasons are bonus tiles. Under the rules used by this project, each scores base points and can also contribute relevant doubles.

### Points are followed by doubles

An ordinary hand can collect a base score and then apply qualifying doubles. Named special hands may instead use fixed values, limit values or their own scoring treatment.

### The losing players can still have scores

This is particularly important when comparing scorers. In British play, a non-winning hand can still have a meaningful score. After paying the winner, non-winners may settle differences between their own scores, with East affecting payments.

That is why a British full-game scorer needs to understand more than the winner's hand.

### Named special hands are part of the tradition

British rules include hands such as Thirteen Unique Wonders, Knitting, Triple Knitting, Wriggling Snake, Imperial Jade, Heads and Tails, Buried Treasure and others. Some also have a specific value when the player is **fishing** — one tile from completion — when somebody else goes Mah-Jong.

## Who might prefer it?

British Mahjong is a natural fit for groups already using BMJA-style rules, particularly social clubs and teaching groups in Britain. It rewards learning how individual parts of an unfinished hand score as well as recognising whole-hand patterns.

**Using British rules?** This site is specifically built for them: score one hand or keep the settlement and history for a complete four-player game.

# Hong Kong Mahjong

## What makes Hong Kong Mahjong distinctive?

Hong Kong Mahjong is a Cantonese tradition with a relatively direct ordinary hand structure. Four melds and a pair remain the normal goal, and multiple Chows are perfectly ordinary.

### Faan rather than British base-points-and-doubles

Hong Kong scoring is generally discussed in terms of **faan/fan**. Patterns add faan, and the resulting total is converted into the applicable table value or payment according to the rules being used.

A minimum hand value is common — three faan is a familiar convention — but this should **not** be presented as universal to every Hong Kong table.

### Chows are normal building blocks

There is no British-style one-Chow cap. If your existing Mahjong experience involves building several sequences without thinking this is unusual, moving to British Mahjong can feel surprisingly restrictive at first.

### Flowers are familiar but conventions vary

Hong Kong sets commonly include Flowers and Seasons, but exact scoring and whether they contribute to a minimum can vary by ruleset/table convention.

### The winner is the scoring focus

Unlike the British system implemented by this project, ordinary Hong Kong settlement is centred on the winning hand rather than calculating every losing player's incomplete hand and then settling differences between them.

## What will a Hong Kong player notice in British Mahjong?

Probably the one-Chow restriction first. Then the explicit base points for exposed/concealed Pungs and Kongs, the detailed scoring of losing hands, British named special hands, and the particular East settlement system.

# Japanese Riichi Mahjong

## What makes Riichi distinctive?

Riichi uses familiar tile groups but adds a tightly codified scoring and defensive framework.

### A complete shape still needs a yaku

Four sets and a pair can form a complete shape, but that alone does not guarantee a legal win. The hand needs at least one recognised **yaku**.

Examples include Riichi itself, fully concealed self-draw, value-tile triplets, All Simples and many more. High-level hands can become yakuman.

### Riichi is an actual declaration

A concealed player in tenpai can declare Riichi and place a bet. The declaration locks the hand in important ways and can become a yaku if the player wins.

### Dora increase value but do not substitute for a yaku

Dora are bonus indicators used to increase hand value. A common beginner trap is to assume having dora makes a hand legally winnable. It does not replace the need for a yaku.

### Furiten changes discard-winning strategy

Riichi's furiten rules can prevent a player from winning on another player's discard even when the tile completes their hand. This creates information and defensive incentives that do not have a British equivalent.

### Standard four-player Riichi does not use Flowers and Seasons

A typical Riichi set uses the 136 core suited and honour tiles rather than the British/Hong Kong/MCR 144-tile set with eight Flowers/Seasons. Red fives may be included as bonus dora depending on the rules in use.

### Scoring uses han and fu

Yaku supply han; the hand also has fu except for special treatments such as seven pairs and limit hands. The payment calculation then depends on the resulting value and dealer/non-dealer status.

## What will a Riichi player notice in British Mahjong?

There is no yaku requirement, no Riichi declaration, no dora indicator system and no furiten. Flowers and Seasons return. Individual Pungs/Kongs can carry British base points, and unfinished losing hands may still produce a score that affects settlement.

# Chinese Official / Mahjong Competition Rules (MCR)

## What is Chinese Official Mahjong?

“Chinese Official” is commonly used in English for **Mahjong Competition Rules (MCR)**. It is also called Chinese International or Guobiao Mahjong in some contexts.

The important distinction is that it is a **standardised competition ruleset**, not a claim that all ordinary Mahjong played in China follows one national household rulebook.

The European Mahjong Association points to the World Mahjong Organization's *Mahjong Competition Rules* (“Green Book”) as the detailed rules reference and uses MCR for recognised tournament play.

### It has a large scoring-combination catalogue

MCR evaluates the winning hand against a standard catalogue of fan/scoring elements with specified values and combination restrictions. It is much more pattern-catalogue-driven than British ordinary point scoring.

### Standard MCR uses an 8-point minimum

A winning hand must reach the competition minimum. This means a structurally complete four-sets-and-pair shape can still be insufficient if it does not contain enough recognised value.

### Multiple Chows are normal

MCR includes many sequence-based patterns, including combinations involving several Chows. There is no British one-Chow cap.

### Flowers are included

MCR uses the 144-tile set including Flowers, although Flowers are treated within MCR's own competition-scoring framework rather than as British 4-point bonus tiles.

## What will an MCR player notice in British Mahjong?

The British catalogue is smaller and differently structured. Ordinary Pungs, Kongs, pairs and Flowers generate base points before doubles; MCR instead asks which standard competition fan are present in the completed winning hand. British losing-hand settlement is another major conceptual difference.

# American Mah Jongg (NMJL-style)

## Why American Mah Jongg is the easiest one to identify

American Mah Jongg is visually related to the same tile family, but its hand-building system is fundamentally different from the other four variants on this page.

### The annual card defines the hands

The National Mah Jongg League publishes a new **Official Standard Hands and Rules** card every year. Players build one of the hands shown on that card.

That means the target catalogue itself changes annually. You cannot learn a permanent British-style list of scoring patterns and assume it defines next year's American game.

### Jokers are part of normal play

American sets add Jokers, which can substitute within specified groupings under the American rules. Jokers are not part of ordinary British, Hong Kong, Riichi or MCR play.

### The Charleston happens before ordinary play

American Mah Jongg begins with the **Charleston**, a structured passing of unwanted tiles between players. None of the other four rulesets compared here has that opening phase.

### Winning hands need not be four sets and a pair

This is the deepest difference. The current card can define combinations using pairs, pungs, kongs, quints, sextets and other arrangements. The card pattern — not the classic four-meld-plus-pair template — tells you what you are trying to complete.

## What will an American player notice in British Mahjong?

There is no annual card, no Charleston and no ordinary Joker mechanism. The target hand is largely built from traditional sets and a pair, with the special-hand catalogue sitting alongside that structure rather than replacing the whole year's legal hand list.

# The biggest differences in practice

## 1. A “legal complete hand” does not mean the same thing everywhere

- **British:** an ordinary legal structure is scored using British points/doubles; named specials have their own conditions.
- **Hong Kong:** a complete hand normally also has to satisfy the minimum value required by the table/ruleset.
- **Riichi:** the player needs at least one yaku; shape alone is not enough.
- **MCR:** the hand must reach the competition minimum through recognised scoring combinations.
- **American:** the tiles must match one of the current card's legal hands.

This is why a generic “Mahjong hand checker” cannot safely support all five simply by changing a scoring table.

---

## 2. Chows tell you a lot about the ruleset

The word Chow describes the same basic three-tile suited sequence, but its strategic role differs enormously.

British Mahjong deliberately restricts an ordinary hand to **one Chow**. Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR allow sequence-rich hands, although their calling and scoring rules differ. American play follows the structures printed on the current card rather than using “how many ordinary Chows can I make?” as the same central question.

---

## 3. Bonus tiles are not universal

British Mahjong uses Flowers and Seasons directly in scoring. Hong Kong commonly uses them, though table rules vary. MCR includes Flowers in its competition framework. Standard four-player Riichi normally removes them entirely. American sets may contain Flower tiles, but their role is governed by the current American card/rules rather than British bonus-tile scoring.

So even before scoring a hand, the physical set on the table can give you clues about the variant.

---

## 4. The scoring philosophies are fundamentally different

### British

Score the pieces of the hand, add relevant bonuses, then apply doubles or recognised special-hand values. Even non-winners may have a score that matters to settlement.

### Hong Kong

Recognised patterns contribute faan/fan and the winning hand's value is settled according to the chosen Hong Kong table/ruleset.

### Riichi

Yaku establish legal scoring value and han, fu refine most non-limit hand values, and table/dealer circumstances determine payments.

### MCR

A standardised catalogue assigns points to recognised fan combinations, with formal combination/exclusion rules and a competition minimum.

### American

The card defines the hand and its value. The primary task is matching the required current pattern rather than calculating British-style base points or Riichi han/fu.

---

## 5. “Dealer” or East does not behave identically

East/dealer matters across several variants, but do not transfer one ruleset's payment rule into another.

In the British model used by this scorer, payments involving East can be doubled and East's win/draw affects seat progression. Riichi also gives dealer hands/payments a higher value and has its own continuation rules. Hong Kong conventions and MCR have their own settlement structures. American play follows its own round/dealer procedures.

“East matters” is shared vocabulary. **How East matters is ruleset-specific.**

# Which Mahjong should I learn?

There is no objectively best version. The most useful answer is usually: **learn the version played by the people you expect to sit down with.**

Choose **British Mahjong** if your club, family or teaching group uses BMJA-style rules and you want to understand traditional British scoring, Flowers/Seasons, specials and full-table settlement.

Choose **Hong Kong Mahjong** if you are joining a Cantonese/Hong Kong-style table and want a relatively direct traditional meld-based ruleset with faan scoring.

Choose **Riichi** if you are attracted to Japanese Mahjong, online competitive play, yaku-building and a strong defensive layer.

Choose **MCR** if your aim is formal Chinese/international competition under a standardised tournament ruleset.

Choose **American Mah Jongg** if the people you want to play with use the NMJL card, Jokers and Charleston. Learning another variant first will help you recognise the tiles, but it will not teach you the current American target hands.

# Can I use the same Mahjong set for all of them?

**Sometimes, but not perfectly.**

British, Hong Kong and MCR commonly use the 144-tile family including Flowers and Seasons. Standard four-player Riichi normally uses 136 tiles and may include red fives, so a general 144-tile set can usually supply the core tiles but may not supply Riichi-specific red fives. American sets add Jokers and are often produced with American-friendly markings/racks, so a plain British/Chinese set will not necessarily contain everything expected for American play.

The safest rule is to check the components required by the specific ruleset before buying a set solely on the word “Mahjong”.

# Frequently asked questions

## Is British Mahjong the same as Hong Kong Mahjong?

No. They share the traditional tile family and many familiar actions, but their scoring and hand rules are different. One conspicuous difference is that an ordinary British hand is limited to one Chow, while Hong Kong play can use multiple Chows. British scoring also gives explicit base points to sets and can score non-winning hands for settlement.

## Is British Mahjong the same as Chinese Mahjong?

“Chinese Mahjong” is too broad to identify one ruleset. China has multiple regional traditions, while MCR/Chinese Official is a standardised competition system. British Mahjong developed its own rules and scoring tradition and should not be assumed equivalent to either Hong Kong play or MCR.

## Is Riichi just Japanese scoring for the same game?

Not really. The core draw/discard and set-building ancestry is recognisable, but Riichi adds game-defining rules including the yaku requirement, Riichi declarations, dora and furiten. These change strategy as well as arithmetic.

## Do all Mahjong games use four sets and a pair?

That is the central ordinary structure in British, Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR, with recognised special exceptions. American Mah Jongg is the major exception in this comparison: its annual card defines the legal target structures, many of which do not follow four ordinary sets plus one pair.

## Which Mahjong uses Jokers?

American Mah Jongg prominently uses Jokers. Ordinary British, Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR rules do not use them as general wild tiles.

## Which Mahjong uses an annual card?

NMJL-style American Mah Jongg. The National Mah Jongg League publishes a new Standard Hands and Rules card each year.

## Which Mahjong uses the Charleston?

American Mah Jongg. It is the pre-play tile-passing phase characteristic of the American game.

## Does Riichi use Flowers and Seasons?

Standard four-player Riichi normally does not. It uses the 136 core suited and honour tiles; many Riichi sets instead include red fives used as bonus dora.

## What is Chinese Official Mahjong?

It is a common English name for Mahjong Competition Rules (MCR), a standardised ruleset used for organised competition. It should not be read as “the one rulebook every Mahjong player in China uses at home”.

## Why can British Mahjong only use one Chow?

That is a rule of the British/BMJA-style ordinary hand. It materially changes hand-building compared with Hong Kong, Riichi and MCR, where multiple sequences are normal. Some named British special layouts contain sequence-like structures but are evaluated as their special pattern rather than as an ordinary multi-Chow hand.

## Can a losing hand score in Mahjong?

It depends on the ruleset. In the British rules used by this project, yes: non-winning hand values can matter when the losers settle differences after paying the winner. That is not the ordinary scoring model in Riichi, MCR or American play.

# Claims and caveats

## Safe strong claims

- British/BMJA-style ordinary hands are limited to one Chow under the rules used by this project.
- British scoring uses base points and doubles and can give non-winning hands scores relevant to settlement.
- Standard four-player Riichi requires a yaku to win.
- Riichi includes Riichi declarations, dora and furiten.
- Standard four-player Riichi normally omits Flowers/Seasons.
- MCR is a standardised competition ruleset and uses an 8-point minimum under the standard rules.
- NMJL-style American Mah Jongg uses an annual card, Jokers and the Charleston.
- NMJL states that it changes the hands/rules card each year.

## Claims that require qualification

- “Hong Kong requires 3 faan.” → say **a three-faan minimum is common/familiar, but exact minimums vary by table/ruleset**.
- “Hong Kong uses Flowers.” → say **commonly**, because variants/table conventions exist.
- “All Riichi sets use red fives.” → false; describe them as common/depending on rules.
- “American Mahjong = NMJL.” → false; explicitly label the comparison NMJL-style American Mah Jongg.
- “MCR is how China plays Mahjong.” → false; distinguish competition standardisation from regional/home play.
- “All Mahjong uses 144 tiles.” → false; Riichi commonly uses 136, American sets add Jokers, and regional variants vary.
- “All Mahjong wins are four sets and a pair.” → false because special forms exist even within Riichi/MCR, and American card structures can be substantially different.
